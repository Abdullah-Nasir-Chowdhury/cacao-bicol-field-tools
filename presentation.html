Bicol Cacao Crisis

From Field to Fix

A pitch grounded in direct interviews and site visits across Albay and the wider Bicol region, August 26–28.

Thesis: every solution we propose already works somewhere in Bicol. Our job is showing how to bring it to cacao.

Nancy

Our probes exposed these problems

Pest control is named problem #1 overall, ahead of typhoon, transport, or post-harvest handling.
Post-harvest loss runs 80–100% from pests/disease network-wide, and near 100% from typhoon in a bad year.
The processing facility is “sometimes out of supply for cacao”: upstream farm losses destabilize downstream production runs.
Power outages are the processing facility's own #1 problem, on top of the supply instability.
Farmer income sits at ₱2,000–10,000/month, with an average farmer age of ~50 and no government succession-training program.
Keisuke

Two fixable problems, not every problem

We scoped deliberately, probing for real problems the farmers themselves called fixable, not inventing new ones.

Out of scope

Typhoon & Mayon ashfall

Every source we interviewed (Muravah, Tatay, the rice/dairy farm, the bamboo farm) called typhoon “unpredictable” and said they'd rather see pests and disease fixed, which is tractable.

Also left alone because it already works: waste management, sanitation, land tenure, and Muravah's transport.

In scope

Flood/drought resilience + pest & disease control

The two problems farmers themselves called fixable, and where a 4× yield recovery is sitting on the table, waiting on pest/disease control specifically.

Guiding principle: adapt, don't invent: every solution ahead is a proven practice from another Bicol farm, retargeted to cacao.

Masa

Path to finished cacao: direct vs. stored

Cumulative days from flowering to a sellable product, per Muravah's stated timeline — flower→pod 6 months, ferment 6 days, solar-dry 1 day–1 week, roast 27 min at 140°C. Storing dried beans 3–6 months before processing roughly doubles the time-to-product. Hover any point for the exact day.

Keisuke

More constraints we have to design around

No annual drought/flood prediction, so Muravah syncs planting to the monsoon and seeks out farmers with existing water access.
Aging farmer population (avg. ~50) with no government training pipeline; Muravah's own coping mechanism is recruiting students from BU Guinubatan.
Hard constraint: no synthetic fertilizers or pesticides, biopesticides/biofertilizers only. Any proposal must fit this.
Explicitly skeptical of AI/robotics: “not there yet, other problems to solve,” and “AI is biased according to the natives.” Solutions must be low-tech, human-in-the-loop.

Claim worth checking

“Cacao will become Bicol's biggest agricultural production.”

Said by a speaker at the Aug 28 youth/agritourism stop (exact attribution unconfirmed; re-check against the raw notes before citing this).

Team's own reaction, not a data point: is that actually achievable given cacao's current production levels? Tatay's own farm sits at ~0 kg/yr actual output against an estimated 2,000 kg/yr potential, and the region-wide post-harvest loss to pests/disease alone runs 80–100%.

Panel orientation: relative energy yield

A single vertical panel collects noticeably less sunlight than an optimally tilted one.

Orientation figures follow the published research on vertical photovoltaics: per-panel, vertical mounting sacrifices direct sunlight versus optimal tilt. The research also shows the recovery path, which is why we flag it rather than dismiss vertical outright: two-sided (bifacial) east–west vertical farms with the right row spacing can yield 10–20% more than conventional monofacial farms per unit of land. Sources: Khan et al., “Vertical bifacial solar farms,” Applied Energy 206 (2017) (free preprint) and Riaz et al., “The optimization of vertical bifacial photovoltaic farms for efficient agrivoltaic systems,” Solar Energy 230 (2021).

Design consideration

Mounting panels vertically wastes potential output: it isn't the efficient way to collect sunlight.

This is the team's own engineering observation (not something a source stated), raised against the Zambales agrivoltaics precedent. The previous slide's orientation chart shows why (~65% vs. 100%).

What we found at Tatay's farm

Pest and disease pressure (not typhoon, not transport, not land tenure) is what's driving Tatay's cacao yield to nearly zero. Continuous, year-round flowering means the pressure never lets up. Disease agent named: Phytophthora (likely P. palmivora, the classic cacao black pod disease); also birds and a viral disease affecting pod production.

Solutions we probed and gathered from other farms

6 of 12 candidate solutions survived independent fact-checking against the interview data. Grouped below by approach, adapted from proven Bicol practices:

Monitoring & routine discipline

  • Cacao-adapted 15-day treatment calendar, from the taro project's proven schedule discipline.
  • Field-officer symptom-logging checklist that feeds Muravah's 500–1,000-farmer data study.
  • Centralized calendar-compliance & bio-input ledger, giving the facility supply visibility.

Biological & cultural control

  • Herbal foliar & pod spray for black pod rot, adapted from the rice/dairy farm's practice.
  • Amino-acid/molasses bio-concoction on a 15-day cycle, from the taro project's recipe.

Post-harvest handling

  • Rolling phytosanitation rounds every 7–14 days: harvest and strip/burn diseased pods in one pass.
Recommended improvements, in order: start with the 15-day calendar and the herbal spray, the cheapest pair that needs no new equipment. Implementation is carried by Muravah's field officers and Bicol University students (the BU Guinubatan pipeline already exists), so the day-to-day lift doesn't fall on Tatay, whose funds and health are already stretched. The compliance ledger and the amino-acid spray follow once the calendar habit is established.

Why this works inside real constraints

1

Matches what farmers actually said they need

Organic-only, low-cost, human-in-the-loop, with no synthetic inputs and no AI/robotics, because that's Muravah and Tatay's explicit requirement.

2

The single largest lever in the dataset

If pest/disease pressure comes down, Tatay's own numbers point to a ~4× recovery: 0 kg to a potential 2,000 kg/year.

3

Fixes two problems at once

Stabilizing farm-level supply also stabilizes the processing facility, currently “sometimes out of supply for cacao.”

4

Nothing invented from scratch

It's the rice/dairy farm's spray practice and the taro project's schedule discipline, adapted to cacao's arboreal, year-round biology.

Keisuke

Finding investors: the gap Tatay named himself

“A pilot study and investors would be needed,” Tatay told us. He can't be expected to raise or run this alone: he farms with limited funds, failing health, and no formal training. The push has to come from Muravah, Bicol University, and its students, with Tatay's farm as the host site. Three realistic paths:

1

The regional funding precedent

The Zambales solar farm raised ₱10M for an agri-infrastructure pilot of comparable scale. Regional investment appetite is proven, and a cacao pilot can be pitched against that benchmark.

2

A pilot inside Muravah's existing study

Joining the 500–1,000-farmer production-cycle study already underway puts an established NGO's track record, and the university's student pipeline, behind the proposal rather than a single farmer's request.

3

The organic price premium as the return story

Framed as bridge capital toward organic and export certification, the proposal pairs a projected 4× yield recovery with a price premium: an investment case with a concrete return, not a request for aid.

~₱200Kone-farm starter pilot: drip starter zone, bio-spray inputs, printed calendars & ledgers, field-officer allowance
₱1.5–2Mten-farm expansion inside Muravah's study, once the starter pilot proves out
₱10Mthe Zambales benchmark: what regional funders have already backed at full scale

Figures are the team's rough estimates, to be refined with Muravah. Possible sources to approach: Department of Agriculture and DOST-PCAARRD programs, provincial LGU agriculture funds, corporate CSR arms already investing in Bicol, development foundations, agri-crowdfunding platforms, and Bicol University research and extension grants.

What we're leaving you with

1

The problem is specific, not vague

Pest and disease pressure, continuous because cacao flowers year-round, has taken Tatay's farm from 500 kg/yr to nearly zero, and drives 80–100% post-harvest losses across the region's cacao network.

2

The fix already exists in Bicol

Six solutions survived fact-checking against our interviews, all adapted from farms that are already succeeding, starting with a 15-day treatment calendar and an herbal spray that need no new equipment.

3

The upside is the region's biggest lever

Resolving the pressure is a projected 4× yield recovery for Tatay alone, and stabilizing farm supply stabilizes the processing facility downstream at the same time.

4

The next step is small and fundable

A bounded pilot: one row, a handful of farms, roughly ₱200K to start, run by Muravah's field officers and Bicol University students, with Tatay's farm as the host site and the investor paths from the previous slide behind it.
